# Visual references for prototype D

For the designer and developers comparing Skyttel's spatial-map backgrounds.
These references help choose recognizable astronomical shapes and distribute
them without competing with the household map. Source review: 2026-10-16.

## Comparison constraints

- Keep A and B unchanged and available as separate options in dark theme.
- Offer separate background choices for light and dark themes; more than one
  successful background can remain available to the user.
- Explore a different palette and treatment for light theme.
- Remove C from the comparison; add D with stars, galaxies and other phenomena.
- D is an artistic background, with invented positions and relative sizes.
  It is not an astronomical chart or a physically scaled simulation.

## Reference vocabulary

- **Spiral galaxy:** a central bulge inside a disk with winding arms. Arms
  provide a distinctive shape beyond a generic glow. **Elliptical galaxy:**
  round to oval, with much less visible internal structure. **Edge-on view:**
  a viewing direction that can hide a spiral galaxy's arms, not another galaxy
  class. Use a thin disk and central bulge as a contrasting silhouette.
  [NASA: Types of galaxies](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/types/)
- **Emission nebula:** glowing gas, often reddish. **Reflection nebula:** dust
  scattering nearby starlight, often bluish. **Dark nebula:** dense dust that
  obscures visible light behind it. These suggest three different treatments:
  irregular luminous clouds, soft blue haze, and patches with fewer visible
  background stars.
  [NASA: Decoding nebulae](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stories/quick-reads/decoding-nebulae/)
- **Open star cluster:** a loose stellar group. **Globular star cluster:** a
  densely packed, roughly spherical collection. Loose and concentrated star
  arrangements can break up an otherwise uniform field without introducing
  another large cloud.
  [NASA: Star clusters](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/star-clusters-inside-the-universes-stellar-collections/)
- **Ring Nebula:** gas shed by a dying star, seen as a textured ring with fainter
  outer arcs. Its projected ring is a useful rare accent.
  [ESA/Webb: Ring Nebula](https://esawebb.org/news/weic2320/)
- **Veil Nebula:** a supernova remnant with intertwined filaments created as
  debris encounters surrounding material. Fine, broken arcs give a different
  silhouette from a smooth ring or broad cloud.
  [NASA: Uncovering the Veil Nebula](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/uncovering-the-veil-nebula/)

## Suggested composition: design inference

The choices below are design proposals, not scientific facts about abundance,
apparent scale or placement. Start with one restrained mixture, then compare
which ingredients deserve more presence.

1. Use mostly small stars, with varied brightness and a few warmer points.
   Avoid making every star a large sparkling cross.
2. Place one recognizable spiral away from the main map objects, a smaller
   edge-on disk elsewhere, and a few very faint elliptical shapes. Vary their
   size, rotation and separation; avoid a repeating wallpaper or evenly spaced
   catalogue of symbols.
3. Let one broad nebula patch contain emission glow, reflection haze and dark
   dust. Reduce stars behind the dust, rather than drawing it as another glow.
4. Add one concentrated cluster and a looser stellar grouping. Keep their
   brightest points below the map objects in visual priority.
5. Use a single faint ring or broken filament accent, away from the main
   spiral. Leave substantial quiet space around household objects and text.
6. Keep positions stable while comparing themes so the comparison isolates
   color and contrast. Avoid animation that makes the background resemble
   moving map objects or incoming notifications.

For dark theme, try a deep blue base, pale stellar points, a restrained warm
galaxy core, and muted blue and rose clouds. For light theme, try an ivory or
cool paper base, tinted stellar halos with light cores, and soft blue, violet
and ochre shapes. Avoid black star dots in light theme.
The light treatment deliberately reads as an illustrated celestial atlas;
simply placing white stars on a pale surface loses their shape. These palettes
need visual comparison with actual map objects before choosing a direction.

## Reading the source colors

Space imagery does not have one universal visible-eye palette. NASA describes
combining filtered monochrome data into color images; infrared wavelengths
can be assigned visible colors in wavelength order. Treat the sources as
references for structure and variety, and label D's colors and composition as
artistic interpretation rather than claiming a faithful telescope view.
[NASA: How Webb and Hubble images are made](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/gravity-assist/gravity-assist-how-we-make-webb-and-hubble-images/)
